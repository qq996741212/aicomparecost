AI Music Generators: Suno vs Udio After 50 Tracks

2026-09-19 · Alex

AI music generation hit a strange milestone: the output is now good enough that the interesting question isn't "can it make music" but "can it make specific music — the kind I actually need, on purpose, without a dozen retries."

I generated fifty tracks across the two leading tools — Suno and Udio — testing the same prompts on both, and tracked what actually differed.

What I Was Testing

The things that matter when you're not just playing around but trying to make something usable:

  • Vocal quality — is the voice convincing, and does it stay in tune?
  • Genre authenticity — does "lofi jazz" actually sound like lofi jazz?
  • Structural control — can you get verse-chorus-verse, or does it wander?
  • Lyrics adherence — does it sing the words you gave it, or paraphrase?
  • The artifacts — the glitches that mark it as AI-generated

The Results

Rather than a scored ranking (both are good and close in quality), here's where the differences actually landed after fifty tracks:

DimensionSunoUdio
Vocal qualityVery good, warmerExcellent, more natural
Genre rangeBroad, reliableBroad, slightly more niche depth
Structural controlGoodBetter, more compositional
Lyrics adherenceGoodGood, occasionally looser
Ease of getting a usable takeHigher on first tryMore retries, higher ceiling
Instrumental/edm stylesStrongVery strong

Where Udio pulled ahead

Vocal naturalness. Udio's vocals were the more convincing — less of the subtle processed quality that betrays synthetic singing. On close listening, Suno's vocals had a slight sheen Udio's often didn't.

Structure and composition. When I wanted an actual song structure — intro, build, breakdown — Udio gave me more control and more musical ideas. It felt more like working with a compositional tool.

Where Suno pulled ahead

Getting a usable take quickly. Suno produced something I could work with on the first try more often. If you want a decent track in one shot, Suno's default quality is slightly more consistent.

Lyrics adherence. Suno stuck to the provided lyrics a bit more faithfully. Udio occasionally took creative liberties — sometimes good, sometimes not what I wanted.

The Artifacts

Both tools have tells. Knowing them is useful whether you're trying to avoid them or (more commonly) just understand what you're hearing:

Vocal artifacts. The occasional slurred word, a slightly unnatural syllable emphasis, or a pitch wobble. Both tools, both reduced over time, but still present at the margins.

The fade-out cop-out. Both tools love to end songs by fading out over repetition, because a fade-out hides the fact that the model doesn't know how to write a real ending. Listen for it — it's the most common structural tell.

Generic lyric drift. When generating lyrics automatically, both drift toward the same themes and the same imagery — neon, shadows, running, dreams. You can spot AI-written lyrics by their favorite metaphors.

The Real Limitation: Specificity

The honest summary after fifty tracks: both tools are great at "make a song in the style of X" and mediocre at "make exactly this specific thing I hear in my head."

Control is the bottleneck, not quality. You can't reliably specify a particular melody, a specific chord change, or an exact vocal line. You can describe a style and a structure, generate several takes, and pick. That's the workflow, and it's a selecting process more than a composing one.

If you need precise musical control, you're better off treating these tools as idea generators and finishing in a real DAW.

What Didn't Work

Expecting exact genre fidelity. Both tools do genres well in broad strokes but often smooth over the specific details that make a genre authentic. "2000s UK garage" produced something close but noticeably smoothed. Plan to describe genres loosely or combine descriptors.

Generating lyrics from scratch. The auto-generated lyrics were the weakest part of the output — generic and thematically samey. Providing my own lyrics improved results substantially.

Expecting one generation to be final. Acceptance was lower than I hoped. The realistic workflow is generating several takes and selecting, not nailing it in one.

Using them for client work without checking terms. The commercial-usage situation differs between the two tools and between free and paid tiers. Read the terms before you sell the output — this bit several people.

Asking for a specific existing artist's sound. This is a legal and ethical line more than a technical one, but worth stating: don't prompt for "a song that sounds like [specific artist]." Both tools increasingly resist it, and it's a rights problem.

Verdict

Both tools are genuinely good, and the choice is mostly about workflow:

  • Suno if you want a usable track fast and value lyrics fidelity.
  • Udio if you want more compositional control, better vocals, and are

willing to do more takes to reach a higher ceiling.

For most people the honest answer is: try both on your actual use case, because your genre and workflow will tip it one way or the other. The quality gap is smaller than the workflow fit.

FAQ

Can I use AI-generated music commercially? It depends on the tool and your tier. Paid tiers generally allow commercial use, but terms differ, and free tiers often don't. Read the specific terms before selling anything.

Which is better, Suno or Udio? Close in quality. Suno is slightly better for getting a usable track on the first try; Udio is slightly better for vocals and compositional control, at the cost of more retries. Your genre and workflow decide.

Can AI music match a specific existing style? It does broad styles well but smooths over genre details. Avoid prompting for specific artists — that's a rights issue — and expect to describe styles loosely.

Are the vocals actually convincing? Increasingly yes, especially on Udio. The remaining tells are subtle — a slurred word, slight pitch wobble, and the characteristic fade-out ending that hides the lack of a real composition.
