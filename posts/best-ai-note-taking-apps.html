Best AI Note-Taking Apps in 2026: Notion AI vs Obsidian vs Mem vs Capacities

2026-10-09 · Alex

The honest answer up front: most "AI" note-taking apps are the same chatbot you already have, bolted onto a notes app you already pay for. The differences that actually matter are the notes app underneath — the AI is mostly a $10/month add-on on top of a subscription you were already weighing.

I know this because I spent a month each living in Notion, Obsidian, Mem, and Capacities, with my full second brain — a few thousand notes, half-finished drafts, meeting notes, and a graveyard of bookmarked articles — imported into all of them. Here's what I'd tell a friend who's trying to decide.

The Question Nobody Asks: Is In-App AI Better Than Pasting Into ChatGPT?

Before you compare apps, compare the category. The killer feature of an AI note app is supposed to be that it reads your notes — your context, your voice, your half-formed thoughts. That's the pitch. In practice:

  • In-app AI wins when it can pull from notes you'd never bother to paste

manually. "What did I decide about the pricing experiment in March?" only works if the app can actually search your history.

  • Plain ChatGPT/Claude wins when the task is just "help me write this

paragraph" or "summarize this article I pasted." You get a frontier model, no per-seat markup, and no vendor lock-in.

If your notes are ten pages of meeting minutes, skip every app on this list and paste into the chatbot you already have. The rest of you, read on.

The Contenders, One Month Each

Notion AI: The Best "Good Enough" Bundle

Notion's AI is the least surprising and the most broadly useful. Ask it a question, it answers using your workspace. Highlight text, it rewrites, expands, or translates. It's the AI feature set most people actually need, executed competently.

What I liked: the retrieval is genuinely useful in a workspace you actually live in. Ask "what's the status of the Q3 project" and it cites pages. That's a real workflow win, not a demo trick.

What grated: it's an add-on on top of an add-on. You pay for the plan, then per-member for AI. Pricing shifts constantly, so treat these as representative and check the site — but as of my last look, AI was roughly the cost of a mid-tier streaming subscription per seat, on top of whatever plan you were already on. For a solo user it stings a little. For a team of twenty, it's a line item.

Obsidian: The One Where You Own Everything

Obsidian is local markdown files on your machine. That's the whole pitch, and it's either exactly what you want or completely uninteresting.

The AI story is different from everyone else here: there is no first-party AI subscription to upsell you. You bring your own — community plugins that call OpenAI, Anthropic, or local models, with your own API key. You pay the provider directly, per token, and you see exactly what it costs.

What I liked: zero lock-in. My notes are files. If Obsidian vanishes tomorrow, my notes are still on my disk, readable in any text editor. The plugins can do retrieval across your vault, and if you run a local model, your notes never leave your machine.

What grated: you are the IT department. Plugin quality swings wildly, plugins break on updates, and "chat with your vault" takes more fiddling than anyone wants to admit. There's real power here, but it costs you Saturday afternoons.

Mem: The One Built Around the AI

Mem is the most interesting bet on this list because the AI isn't a feature layer — the whole app is organized around what the AI can find. Notes don't need folders; you write, and the AI surfaces connections later.

What I liked: for people who hate organizing, this is the best "just write and find it later" experience I used. Zero taxonomy maintenance is a genuine pleasure.

What grated: when the AI misfiles something, there's often no folder to check as a fallback — you search, or you don't find it. And the pricing model has changed more than once during the time I've watched it, so read the current page before budgeting.

Capacities: The Structured-Thinking One

Capacities is the deep-cut pick. It's object-based: a book is a Book object, a person is a Person object, and everything links up in a typed graph. If Obsidian's philosophy is "files and links," Capacities' is "objects and relations."

What I liked: for research-heavy work — reading lists, source notes, anything where you're building a personal knowledge base out of distinct things — the structure pays for itself. The AI assistant queries across typed objects, which means it can answer questions like "what did I note from books by this author" in a way freeform markdown can't.

What grated: you have to buy into the object metaphor. If you just want a place to dump thoughts, this is more discipline than you're going to maintain.

Reflect: The Fast, Simple, Daily-Notes One

Reflect deserves a mention as the anti-Notion: fast, minimal, centered on daily notes, with an AI add-on that does the expected rewrite-and-chat tricks.

What I liked: it opens fast, syncs fast, and stays out of your way. After a month of Notion's loading states, "instant" is a feature.

What grated: deliberately limited. If you need databases, you're in the wrong place.

What Didn't Work

Asking any of them to synthesize across thousands of old notes. They all do fine on a dozen recent notes. Push past that and the retrieval gets fuzzy in ways that are hard to spot — confidently wrong summaries of things you wrote six months ago. Verify anything important.

Paying for AI in a second app I already had elsewhere. Midway through testing I realized I was paying for AI features in a notes app while still opening ChatGPT for anything hard. If you already subscribe to a chatbot, check whether you'd actually use the in-app version before adding the line item.

Trusting the rewrite features with my own voice. Every app's "improve this" button made my writing sound like marketing copy. Fine for a first draft, never for a final one. I stopped using it for anything client-facing.

Thinking the AI would make me organized. None of these replace a system. If your notes are chaos, AI search makes chaos searchable — which is useful, but it's not the same as structure.

Verdict

For most people: Notion AI. Not because it's the most exciting — it's the most boring, and that's the point. It does the expected things reliably, in an app you probably already use, and the workspace-aware retrieval is the one AI feature here that changes a daily workflow instead of just being a demo.

If ownership and privacy are non-negotiable: Obsidian. Your notes are files, your AI is your own API key, nothing leaves your machine if you don't want it to. You'll tinker more, but you'll never be locked in.

If you specifically hate organizing: Mem. The AI-first, no-folders approach is a real and rare pleasure — just accept that a misfiled note can be hard to find.

If you're building a research knowledge base: Capacities. The typed-object model is meaningfully better than flat markdown for that job.

My own setup ended up as Obsidian for everything I need to keep forever, plus a chatbot subscription for the heavy lifting. That split costs less than any two AI note apps and owns less of my data — but it only works because I don't mind being my own sysadmin. If you'd rather not, take Notion.

FAQ

Is the AI in note apps worth it if I already pay for ChatGPT or Claude? Only if you'll regularly ask questions that require searching your notes — past decisions, project history, things you wrote down and forgot. If your usage is "help me write," the chatbot you already have covers it and the add-on is redundant.

What about privacy — do these apps train on my notes? It varies by provider and changes as policies get updated, so read the current terms for anything sensitive. Obsidian with your own API key (especially a local model) is the safest option here by a wide margin. Never paste confidential client or health information into any cloud AI tool.

Can I try these before paying? Yes — all of them have free tiers or trials, though the AI features are often capped or excluded on free plans. Take a month on the free tier before you hand over a credit card.

Which one is cheapest long-term? Obsidian with a local model is essentially free after you've got the hardware. After that, it depends entirely on how many seats you need and which features you actually use — the add-on math changes the ranking fast, so re-check prices every few months.
